Chapter 1 · about 8500–7500 BCE · Anatolia, the Near East

The stone that would not chip

For more than two and a half million years, people and their ancestors struck stone against stone. Then someone struck a piece that behaved quite differently.

The chapter in a few words. Hit copper, and it dents instead of splitting. Its atoms, the tiny balls that make up matter, can slide in rows and still hold together. But each blow leaves trapped “folds” in those rows. These make the metal harder. Eventually, it cracks.

Step 1 · What people noticed

A dent instead of a chip

In the Stone Age, a key skill was knowing how to strike flint, a stone, to knock off a sharp flake. Everyone did this, every day. So when someone found an unfamiliar stone, hitting it was a natural thing to try.

Sometimes a riverbed or a rock face held a heavy, reddish lump with a green crust. This was native copper: pure metal found ready-made in nature. Picture someone striking it and expecting a flake to break off. Nothing breaks away. The blow leaves a dent.

To that person, this looked like a stone with a new property. You could hammer it into a shape, and it would keep that shape. No stone could do that.

Why copper can be found as metal. Almost all metals in the ground are joined to other substances. They look like ordinary rock. Almost any stone contains a little metal. We call a rock ore only when it contains enough to make extracting the metal worth the work. Copper, gold and silver do not readily join other substances in this way. So, occasionally, they occur pure. The chemical symbol for copper is Cu. It comes from the Latin word cuprum, “metal from Cyprus”.
Step 2 · What people learned to do

Cold forging

That discovery became a craft. Put a piece of copper on a flat stone and strike it with another stone. Flatten it into a sheet. Roll the sheet into a little tube, and you have a bead. Or hammer the copper into an awl, a pointed tool for making holes, a hook or a pin. This needs no fire. Shaping metal with blows without heating it is called cold forging.

The best-known early collection comes from the settlement of Çayönü Tepesi, in what is now southeastern Turkey. It contains about 113 beads, hooks, awls and pins made from native copper. They date to 8200–7500 BCE. Some finds may be older: copper beads from Aşıklı Höyük, possibly from 8500 BCE or earlier, and a pendant from northern Iraq, dated to about 8700 BCE.

People near the Great Lakes in North America made the same discovery independently. Wood and cord left on their copper objects date from 8500 to 3580 years ago. They forged the copper and used annealing, heating it to make it soft again. Archaeologists still debate whether they also melted it and cast it, pouring the melted metal into a shape.

The order of events is less clear. The usual story puts cold forging first and heating later. Yet the objects from Çayönü already show signs of heating between rounds of hammering. So far, the finds reveal no clear period when people only forged copper cold. Perhaps that period was short. Perhaps its objects have simply not been found. We study cold forging on its own because it is a separate physical process. This is not a claim about which came first.

The craft brought two lessons. The more you hammer a piece, the harder it gets. That is useful: an awl made from hammered copper is sharper and does not bend. But every blow also brings the piece closer to cracking at the edges. Then the work must be thrown away.

Step 3 · How people explained it then

They left no written explanation

Writing was still almost five thousand years away. The first tablets from Uruk date to 3350–3200 BCE. So we have no explanations from the people who first worked copper. We do not know what they thought it was. It is plausible that they saw it as a special kind of stone. But that is a guess, not a fact.

The objects themselves tell us how people worked. Cut an ancient bead open and look at the cut surface through a microscope. The pattern in the metal shows whether it was hammered and whether it was heated afterwards. We will return to the microscope in chapter nine. For now, we will look deeper than it can show us.

Step 4 · A look inside

What a blow does inside the metal

Meet the atom

All matter is made of atoms. For now, picture them as very small balls. In copper and other metals, they do not sit in a jumble. They form neat rows, like oranges stacked on a stall. This regular arrangement is called a crystal. Not every solid has it. In glass, for example, the pattern of atoms does not repeat over long distances.

In copper, neighbouring atoms are 0.256 nanometres apart. A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. About four million copper atoms fit in a row one millimetre long. The balls in the pictures below are enlarged tens of millions of times.

Why stone splits and copper dents

The difference lies in what holds those tiny balls together.

Flint is made of quartz, a substance in which silicon and oxygen are joined together: SiO2. Each atom is held to particular neighbours by stiff links, called bonds. Imagine little rods between them. Slide one layer past another, and the rods break. They do not join up again. A crack has formed.

In metal, the atoms are not tied to particular neighbours. A shared “glue” spreads between them all and holds them together. In chapter six, we will meet the electrons that provide this glue. Move a layer by one step. Each atom now has a new neighbour, but the glue is still there. This way of holding atoms together is called metallic bonding.

What is true, and what is simplified. In metal, bonds are not tied to particular neighbours. They form again after a layer slides. In stone, the bonds point in particular directions and do not form again once broken. Those differences are real. The picture is simplified: real crystals have depth, and the lattice, the repeating arrangement of atoms, is more complex in quartz than a square grid. A real stone does not split because a whole layer slides at once. A crack starts at a small flaw and runs through the piece, breaking bonds one after another. Nor does a whole layer move at once in copper. The next experiment shows why.

Try releasing the slider before the halfway point. The copper layer moves back. This return to its old shape is called elasticity. It is why a copper sheet springs back. Release the slider after halfway, and the layer jumps forward by one step and stays there. That is the dent. In real copper, the movement that can spring back is much smaller than half a step. The next section explains why.

The layer moves a little at a time

Moving a whole layer of atoms at once would take an enormous force. For copper, the calculation gives several gigapascals, units that measure force per area. Yet real, pure metal starts to flow under a load about a thousand times smaller. Something must make movement easier.

Try pulling a large carpet across the floor by its edge. It is hard work. Now make a fold and push it from one end to the other. The carpet moves by the width of the fold with almost no effort. Something similar happens in a crystal. A small “fold” travels through it: a place with an extra row of atoms. At any moment, only one line of bonds breaks and joins up again.

The scientific name for this fold is a dislocation. It is what makes the metal soft.

Why hammering makes copper harder

Every blow creates new folds. When there are few, they can travel freely through the metal, and the piece changes shape easily. But folds get in each other’s way. Two meet on neighbouring rows and lock together. A third runs into them. A fourth runs into the third.

With its folds trapped, the metal is harder to reshape: nothing is free to move. You can feel the difference through the hammer. This strengthening by blows is called work hardening. Copper hardened in this way is about twice as hard as soft copper. A native copper axe from the Great Lakes was left as it was after cold forging. Its hardness is 116 on the Vickers scale, a scale for measuring hardness. A spearhead heated in a fire after forging measures 59. When folds have almost nowhere to go, the metal can no longer give way to a blow by changing shape. The load builds up in the most crowded places, and cracks open there.

There is a precise rule: strength grows as the square root of the density of folds, meaning how closely they are packed. Put simply, to double the extra strength, you need four times as many folds.

A piece of metal is made of grains

A piece of metal is almost never one crystal. It consists of many small crystals joined tightly together. In each, the rows of atoms point in a different direction. Each small crystal is called a grain. The place where two grains meet is a grain boundary.

What is true, and what is simplified. Metal really does contain grains whose rows point in different directions. The sizes are simplified. Copper grains range from hundredths of a millimetre to almost a millimetre. Measured ranges include 0.02–0.08 mm and 0.03–0.7 mm. So a real grain has tens of thousands to millions of atoms across it, not the few dozen shown here. The atoms at the boundaries are only a rough sketch.

Where folds come from, and why they travel both ways

A hammer does not push a fold as your hand pushes a fold in a carpet. A blow squeezes the whole piece. Throughout it, a force tries to slide the layers of atoms past each other. This effect is strongest on planes at an angle to the blow.

Folds come in two kinds, called opposite signs. One has its extra half-row above it; the other has it below. The same force drives folds of opposite signs in opposite directions. That is why folds in the lens move both left and right, although the hammer strikes from one side.

When the metal first starts to change shape, folds mostly begin at uneven places on grain boundaries. They also start at the surface of the piece. But this matters little in a metal made of many grains: most grains do not touch the surface. Inside a grain, there are sources that release a fold as a closed loop under load. The loop expands in every direction. Our picture is a flat slice. A loop crosses that slice in two places, so it looks like two folds of opposite signs moving apart. Between them, the layer has already shifted by one atom.

Moving folds are more than a helpful picture. In 1956, the researchers Hirsch, Horne and Whelan recorded dislocations moving through thin aluminium foil with an electron microscope. They saw the motion directly.

The lens below shows a small patch inside one grain. Its meters show hardness and ductility, the ability to change shape without cracking. Grain boundaries also block folds. In the next grain, the rows point a different way, so a fold cannot simply carry on. We will look more closely at grains in chapters 2 and 9.

Step 5 · What it made possible, and what stood in the way

A hard awl and a cracked piece of copper

Long before copper, people shaped stone, bone and wood by taking material away. They chipped, cut or ground it off. Cold forging gave them a strong material they could reshape with blows, losing nothing. They could flatten it into a sheet, draw it out into a rod or bend it into a hook. Work hardening made an object harder without adding anything. People deliberately hammered the working edge of an awl or blade.

But work hardening also set a limit. You could not forge a large object from a nugget: the copper would become hard and split before it reached the right shape. The craft needed a way to make hammered copper soft again.

Remember
  • Metal is made of atoms in regular rows. A shared “glue” holds them together, rather than bonds to particular neighbours.
  • The rows can slide while the metal stays in one piece. Stone cannot do this, so it splits.
  • A row does not move all at once. A fold, or dislocation, travels through it. While folds can move freely, the metal is soft.
  • Hammering creates many folds. They lock together and become trapped. The metal becomes harder through work hardening, then cracks.

Fire makes copper soft again

A piece of copper has become hard and is close to cracking. After a spell in the fire and time to cool, it can be shaped again, like a fresh nugget. We do not know how people discovered this. But even the objects from Çayönü show signs of heating. What does fire do to the folds?
