Metal: from nugget to single crystal

For ten thousand years, people have been learning to work metal. This book follows their discoveries and the changes inside the metal. Each chapter begins with something people noticed. Then we try their method, explore what they thought was happening, and look inside to see what really happens.

  1. 1 about 8500–7500 BCE
    Anatolia, the Near East

    The stone that would not chip

    Native copper dents under a blow. Then it hardens and cracks. Meet atoms, the tiny balls that make up matter.

  2. 2 about 8200–7500 BCE
    the same region

    Fire makes copper soft again

    Annealing makes metal soft again. See what heat does to folds in the rows of atoms, and what temperature tells us about their movement. Learn how to anneal well.

  3. 3 about 5000 BCE
    south-eastern Europe, Asia Minor

    Metal from stone

    Charcoal helps turn green stone into copper. See why heat alone is not enough, and how liquid metal becomes a solid full of grains. Learn to cast a shape without holes.

  4. 4 3300–1200 BCE

    Bronze

    Bronze is a mixture of metals. Why can mixing two soft metals give something harder than either one?

  5. 5 from 1200 BCE

    Iron from the furnace

    A furnace is an oven built for very high heat. Here it produces a lump of iron that people shape with a hammer, without melting it.

  6. 6 antiquity and the Middle Ages

    Steel, hardening and tempering

    Steel is iron with added carbon, the substance in charcoal. Hardening makes it harder; tempering makes hardened steel less likely to break. Two ways to make patterned steel: melting it in a pot, or joining layers of metal.

  7. 7 Bohemia, 1403

    A smith’s workshop in the time of the game Kingdom Come

    Wheels driven by water, large furnaces for melting iron, iron poured into shapes, and groups of craftspeople who share a trade.

  8. 8 sixteenth–eighteenth centuries

    From alchemy to chemistry

    From alchemy, with its attempts to turn one metal into another, to chemistry, the study of substances and how they change. Testing what is in a metal, changing ideas about burning, and finding oxygen and carbon.

  9. 9 nineteenth century

    Rivers of steel, and metal under the microscope

    Making steel in large amounts. A microscope magnifies the view of the metal’s tiny grains, each a region of ordered atoms. A chart shows how iron changes with temperature and the amount of carbon in it.

  10. 10 twentieth century and beyond

    Designing the structure

    Looking inside metal with X-rays, a kind of light that can pass through it. Understanding folds in the rows of atoms and making steel that resists rust. Blades for rotating engines, each made as one crystal: a single region of regularly arranged atoms.
